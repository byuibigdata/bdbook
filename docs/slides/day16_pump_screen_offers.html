One Prediction on the Pump Screen

What to show a customer while the tank fills — and how we would know it worked

J. Hathaway

The ask

Ninety seconds. One screen. One offer.


The customer is watching the gallons tick. What do we put in front of them, and how did we decide?

What the screen knows

The card is read before the fuel flows, so at the moment of the offer we have:

Context

Store, pump, fuel grade, dollars pumped so far, and the hour — in store wall-clock time.

Identity

A shopper id from the card. Every transaction in our extract has one.

History

Whatever that shopper did on previous visits — if there were any.

Anything we can’t know before the nozzle goes back is not a feature. That is the leakage rule with a clock on it.

Where we are going

  • 1 The label. Anchor on fuel, sweep −15/+45, drop the card fees. There is no column for “did they come inside?” — we have to build one.
  • 2 What the data says. Where the signal is, and where it is not. This decides what the models are even allowed to be.
  • 3 Two models. Which category on probability, then which item inside it — plus the checks that say both are working.
  • 4 The economics. Who funds the discount, and when the answer is to show nothing.
  • 5 The second item, which is the one actually worth discounting.

Step 1 is the one everything else inherits. Get the label wrong and every number after it is confident and false.

Part 1 · The label

The label does not exist. We build it.

We want to predict “will this person come inside and buy something?” No column says that. The pump and the counter are two separate transactions — fuel at 10:41, coffee at 10:46 — tied together only by the shopper id and the clock.

So we anchor a window on the fuel transaction and sweep up everything that shopper bought nearby:

−15 min

shopped, then filled

→

FUEL

the anchor

→

+45 min

filled, then shopped

Asymmetric on purpose: most people fuel first, and 45 minutes after leaves room for a queue. This is a unit-of-analysis decision, and it is ours to defend — every number in this deck inherits it.

A trap in the label

First run said 19.4% of fuel visits bought something inside. The top category was SERVICES, at 40% of all conversions.

What SERVICES actually is

99.5% card-processing fees. debitfee, Debit Fee, Card Fee — $0.35, charged on the fuel sale itself.

The fuel transaction was leaking into its own label. A model trained on that learns “customers who pay by card buy SERVICES” — perfectly accurate, perfectly useless.

Look at what our positive class is made of before we train on it. Removing the fees moved the answer by seven points.

The label, honestly

36.1M fuel visits

12.8% buy something inside

$7.87 median in-store spend when they do

About 1 in 8. That is the number every model below has to beat, and the number that sets how much a discount can be worth.

940 stores across 8 states, October 2024 to September 2026 — that is the exploration set. The models are fitted on a pinned 24-store pilot (2.4M visits); scaling to all 940 needs the store identifier re-encoded, because gradient boosting caps a categorical at 255 levels. Every figure comes from that extract unless labelled an assumption.

How we judge ourselves

One date decides whether any number in this deck is worth anything.

Window Fuel visits
Train Oct 2024 → 31 Mar 2026 1.85M
Validate 1 Apr 2026 → Sep 2026 584k

Never a random split

Shuffle these rows and a model learns from next month to predict last month. It scores beautifully and the score is uncollectable — nobody can buy a prediction that needed the future to make it.

The cutoff binds everything, not just the classifiers. The recommender is factored on purchases before that date, and the complement table is built from baskets before it. Both are easy to forget — they feel like preparation rather than modelling, so they are where the future leaks back in.

Features are point-in-time too: a shopper’s history on any fill counts only the visits strictly before it. Every held-out figure quoted later — the calibration, the hit rates, the second-item result — comes from those six months the models never saw.

Part 2 · What the data says

The sale happens fast

When, relative to the pump Share of conversions
−15 to 0 min — shopped first 16.2%
0 to 5 min 77.2%
5 to 15 min 5.3%
15 to 45 min 1.3%

Two things follow, and both shape the product:

  • The offer must appear during the fill. Three in four decisions are made within five minutes of the nozzle going back.
  • Attach runs 7.5% at 2am and 15.1% at 6am — a 2× spread on the clock alone, which is why the hour is in every model below.
  • The 16.2% who shopped first have already converted — an offer on the screen cannot cause a basket they bought ten minutes ago. We keep those visits: the basket is real evidence about what goes with a tank of gas, and it is the training signal either way. What an offer can win there is a second trip inside, which happens on under 5% of those fills — upside, not the core of the case.

But what they buy is concentrated

Of the visits that do convert:

Category Share
Packaged Beverages (non-alcoholic) 54.5%
Cigarettes 14.7%
Candy 13.5%
Salty Snacks 13.1%
Other Tobacco / Nicotine 12.6%
Beer 10.5%

Six categories cover almost everything. This is a small problem, not a 35-class one — and it is the part of the question where a person’s past actually matters.

Part 3 · The models

Two models, named for the method

Category Relevance

Will they buy a drink on this fill?

Classification — six calibrated binary classifiers (GBT).

SKU Affinity

Which drink?

ALS matrix factorisation on implicit feedback.

Best-Seller

The popularity fallback — and the path for ~68% of fills.

Offer Economics

Not a model. The two above find the item they buy anyway, which needs no discount — so the money goes on the second item, priced under a funding share.

Relevance picks the category. Affinity picks the item inside it — on probability, never on margin, because an assumed 40% should not get to choose what we show.

Category Relevance · What will they buy?

Six binary classifiers — one per category, fitted over every fuel visit, not only the ones that converted.

Not a multiclass model

A basket holds several categories at once; our six shares sum to 119%, not 100%. A softmax would force them to compete and return numbers we can’t price.

Each model answers one question

P(buys beer on this fill), P(buys a drink on this fill), … — independently, so each is a real probability for its offer. Walking inside is already inside that number.

Where the person enters

Per category, from visits before this one: bought it before? what share of prior baskets? how long since?

GBTClassifier here — this is where interactions live. Beer on a Friday evening.

Does the customer id earn its place?

Fit every category twice — context alone, then context plus who they are — and compare on the held-out period:

Category Context + customer Gain
Cigarettes 0.667 0.884 +0.217
Other Tobacco / Nicotine 0.617 0.838 +0.221
Packaged Beverages 0.642 0.750 +0.109
Beer 0.764 0.870 +0.106
Salty Snacks 0.599 0.695 +0.096
Candy 0.643 0.726 +0.083

Identity does almost nothing for whether they come in, and almost everything for what they buy — most of all for the habitual categories. That asymmetry is why the customer id is worth the engineering it costs.

Out-of-time AUC from an ablation on converting visits, 218,657 train / 78,259 test.

SKU Affinity · which item inside the category?

A category is not an offer. “Buy a beverage” sells nothing; “$1 off a 16oz Monster” does. But only where the tail is long enough to be worth narrowing:

Category SKUs Top 100 covers
Packaged Beverages 4,049 45.1%
Candy 3,902 43.3%
Cigarettes 1,311 85.9%

Beverages leaves most of the distribution on the table if you show only the best seller. Cigarettes does not — decide per category by looking at the tail.

The objection to a recommender here is sparsity: the median shopper has two transactions. Conditioning on the category fixes it. Of the 2.75M who bought a beverage, 55% bought three or more — people repeat items rather than sampling, and a repeated choice is a preference worth factoring.

The baseline to beat is the store’s own best seller, not random.

ALS has no clock, so we put the time in the key

ALS takes no features — it cannot know it is 6am. But the item can carry the hour: “Red Bull@morning” becomes its own item.

Daypart changes what sells — share of beverage purchases:

Brand Morning Midday Evening
Red Bull 37.9% 30.3% 25.1%
Monster 32.0% 22.3% 18.6%
Coca-Cola 6.2% 12.5% 15.6%

And it holds inside one person — every pair of purchases by the same shopper:

The pair Same SKU
Same daypart 18.0%
Different daypart 10.0%

The left table alone could just be different people shopping at different times. The right one is the control: hold the person fixed and same-daypart pairs are still 80% more likely to be the same item.

Three dayparts, not four — morning 5–10 (21.8% of visits), midday 10–3 (35.2%), evening 3–5am (43.1%). Afternoon and night behave almost identically, so a fourth bucket would fragment the data for nothing.

Does it work? Two models, two different checks

We said calibration is the whole job, so here is the calibration. Every 583,583 fill in the held-out period, sorted by predicted P(buys a beverage) and cut into ten equal buckets:

Decile Predicted Actual Gap
1 (lowest) 0.0001 0.0005 +0.0004
5 0.0385 0.0395 +0.0009
9 0.1400 0.1407 +0.0008
10 (highest) 0.3430 0.3287 −0.0144

The middle is calibrated to within a tenth of a point. The top decile is the one to watch — the model says 34.3%, the customers deliver 32.9%, so it is mildly over-confident exactly where we are most willing to spend. An offer priced off that decile buys about 4% less attach than the arithmetic assumes.

Stage one is judged on calibration because its output is multiplied by dollars. A model that ranks well but is scaled wrong prices every offer wrong, and AUC would never show it.

The full audit — ten real customers, raw data to prediction to outcome — is at One Customer, End to End.

And did stage two name the right item?

Calibration says nothing about the SKU. The direct test: when a customer bought something in the category we offered, was the item they bought in our top k?

k SKU Affinity Store best seller Lift
1 — what the screen shows 9.1% 3.2% 2.8×
5 24.2% 11.4% 2.1×
10 34.3% 16.9% 2.0×

25,894 held-out fills where ALS ran. Both rankings scored on the same rows — comparing ALS rows against fallback rows would compare two populations and flatter ALS for reasons unrelated to the model. Across all fills, personalised and fallback together, hit@1 is 6.2% against 2.9%.

Where it earns most

Beer 3.1×, Candy 2.3×, Beverages 1.9×. The longer the tail, the more ALS is worth.

The control that matters

On fallback rows the two rankings agree 100.000% of the time — they are the same ranking by construction. If that ever drifts, the harness is broken.

The offer worth funding is the second item

The model above finds what they were going to buy anyway. Discounting that is the one promotion certain to cost margin and change nothing.

So ask the other question. 46.5% of drink baskets are drink-only — that is the addressable population: someone buying a drink regardless, who might add something.

Second category Their rate Equally frequent others Index Excess
Alternative Snacks 6.0% 3.5% 1.74 2.6 pts
Commissary 3.2% 1.9% 1.64 1.2 pts
Packaged Sweet Snacks 6.7% 4.7% 1.41 2.0 pts
Salty Snacks 12.2% 9.4% 1.30 2.8 pts
Candy 12.2% 11.4% 1.07 0.8 pts

Candy is the trap

Candy is 18.2% of every second item in a drink basket, which makes it look like the obvious pair. Drink regulars buy it 1.07× as often as shoppers who come in just as much and don’t buy drinks. It is not a complement — it is just everywhere.

Compared against shoppers who come in just as often but don’t buy the anchor — without that control every cohort indexes high on everything, because every cohort is a cohort of heavy shoppers.

And this is the one claim we cannot settle

Scored on held-out data, our pair offer lands 10.8% against 14.0% for simply picking the most popular second category. That is by design — where a shopper already buys salty snacks constantly we steer elsewhere — but it means we trade a 26% chance of naming something they’d have bought anyway for a 4.5% chance on something incremental. Only a holdout can say which is worth more.

What we cannot claim

Every number above is a prediction, not a causal effect. We have shown what people do, not what an offer would change.

The gap

A high score says this person buys drinks. It does not say a discount makes them buy one. Nothing in our data separates the two.

What exists

The discounts table has real promotions — loyalty, mix & match, retailer promo — with observable outcomes.

Why it isn’t enough

Assignment wasn’t random. Stores promoted what they expected to sell, so the comparison is biased.

The one thing we need from you: a holdout. Some pumps show no offer. Without it, uplift is not measurable — by us or by anyone.

Could we measure it from what we have?

The discounts table records redemptions, not impressions — 9.2M discounts applied, and zero records of an offer shown and declined. P(buy | offered) has no denominator.

Stores switched promotions on at different dates, though, and that stagger is a natural experiment. Monster’s share of beverage lines gives a clean +1.7 points for treated stores.

We should not believe it

Store count goes 49 → 280 at week zero: almost nobody has a pre-period. The rising pre-trend is different stores entering the average, not behaviour changing — and the share was climbing before the promo and plateaus after, the opposite of the shape a causal effect makes.

The design is sound. This estimate is not. A balanced panel of late adopters would fix it — and that is a better argument for the holdout than any amount of methodology.

The build order

  • 1 The visit table. Anchor on fuel, sweep −15/+45, drop the card fees. Everything below depends on this being right.
  • 2 Category Relevance. Six calibrated models. The best-seller fallback ships with it, because it carries ~68% of fills.
  • 3 SKU Affinity where the tail justifies it.
  • 4 The expected value rule, priced against showing nothing — and the funding share that decides who pays.
  • 5 The second item. The complement table, which is where a discount can actually change a basket.
  • 6 The holdout, then uplift in dollars.

Steps 1 to 3 are the engineering. Steps 4 to 6 are where the money is — and step 6 is the one we cannot do without you.
