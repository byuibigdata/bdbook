What to Show on the Pump Screen

The setting

Ninety seconds. One screen. One offer.


The customer is standing at the pump watching the gallons tick. What do you put in front of them, and how did you decide?

It’s a decision, not a prediction

A model that says this shopper has a 23% chance of buying a drink hasn’t decided anything yet.

Predict

How likely is each outcome?

Value it

What is each outcome worth, minus what the offer costs?

Choose

Show the one offer with the highest expected value.

Expected value = P(accepts) × margin − cost of the discount. Every method below is a way to get that first number.

Five methods

1 · Association rules

“People who buy this also buy that.”

  • No model to train, no cold start
  • Works on the first visit, with no shopper ID
  • Explainable to a store manager in one sentence

The limit

It describes the basket, not the person. Everyone buying fuel sees the same offer.

Reference: Baskets of Goods →

2 · Segment-based offers

Cluster the shoppers, then write one offer per segment.

  • Stable: segments don’t move week to week
  • Explainable: morning commuters get coffee, fill-up-only gets a car wash
  • A human can sanity-check every offer before it ships

The limit

Everyone in a segment gets the same thing, and your segments were built to describe shoppers, not to predict what they’d accept.

Reference: Customer Segmentation →

3 · Propensity models

One model per offer: P(buys this category | what we know at the pump).

  • Ranks offers by probability for this visit
  • Uses the features you’ve been building: fuel grade, gallons, daypart, history
  • Scales to as many candidate offers as you have models

The limit

It predicts what they’d do anyway. Your best-scoring offer is often the thing they were already going to buy.

Reference: Labeled Baskets →

4 · Uplift models

Don’t predict the purchase. Predict the change in purchase.

Persuadable

Buys only if shown the offer. The only group worth paying for.

Sure thing

Buys either way. The discount is pure margin loss.

Lost cause

Never buys. Wasted screen.

Sleeping dog

Buys unless you bother them. Rare, but real.

You need a holdout — some customers get no offer — or you can never measure uplift.

5 · Contextual bandits

Learn while serving. Mostly show the offer you believe is best, and sometimes try another to keep learning.

  • Handles a changing offer lineup without retraining from scratch
  • Solves the cold-start problem by exploring
  • Measures itself as it runs

The limit

Hardest to build, hardest to explain to a client, and it needs real-time serving at the pump.

What our data supports

The honest constraints

29.6% of transactions carry a shopper ID — and it’s card-based

52% of shoppers appear more than once in two years

12.2% of fuel visits include an in-store purchase

Roughly seven in ten visits are anonymous to us. A personalization plan that assumes a known shopper fails most of the time.

Example conclusions — from one run on the Idaho sample, not a finished analysis.

A fallback ladder

pump, hour, day, weather, fuel grade

→

Recognized card

→

Known history

propensity, then uplift

Build the anonymous tier first. It covers most visits, needs no identity, and gives you the baseline that everything else has to beat.

Where this meets your features

The pump screen only knows what you can compute before the customer walks in:

  • Fuel grade, gallons so far, payment type, pump number
  • Hour, weekday, store
  • For recognized shoppers: visit count, days since last visit, past mission mix

Anything you can’t know at the moment of the offer is not a feature. That’s the same leakage rule, with a clock on it.

Presenting a method to a client

They don’t want the algorithm. They want the decision and the risk.

  1. The decision you’re automating, in one sentence
  2. Why this method fits the data you actually have
  3. What it costs to be wrong — a bad offer, a wasted discount, an annoyed customer
  4. How you’d know it worked: holdout, lift over no-offer, measured in dollars
  5. What you’d build first — the simplest version that beats the baseline
