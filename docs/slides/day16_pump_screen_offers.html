One Prediction on the Pump Screen

Day 16: Designing the model from the data up

J. Hathaway

Today

We build one thing, end to end: a prediction we can put on the pump screen.

Step 1

Build a label that doesn’t exist. No column says “did they come inside?”

Step 2

Read what the data says — and what it refuses to say.

Step 3

Three models, then turn a probability into an offer in dollars.

And at the end, the one thing we have to ask the client for.

The ask

Ninety seconds. One screen. One offer.


The customer is watching the gallons tick. What do we put in front of them, and how did we decide?

What the screen knows

The card is read before the fuel flows, so at the moment of the offer we have:

Context

Store, pump, fuel grade, dollars pumped so far, and the hour — in store wall-clock time.

Identity

A shopper id from the card. Every transaction in our extract has one.

History

Whatever that shopper did on previous visits — if there were any.

Anything we can’t know before the nozzle goes back is not a feature. That is the leakage rule with a clock on it.

Where we are going

  • 1 The visit table. Anchor on fuel, sweep −15/+45, drop the card fees. Everything below depends on this being right.
  • 2 Model A. Context plus their own attach rate. A calibrated P(inside).
  • 3 Model B on converters. Six binary models, six categories.
  • 4 Model C where the tail justifies it. ALS inside beverages first.
  • 5 The expected value rule — including the decision to show nothing.
  • 6 Ask for the holdout, then measure uplift in dollars.

Steps 1 and 2 are a semester’s worth of real work. Today is step 1 — and it is the one everything else inherits.

Step 1 · The label

There is no label in the data

We want to predict “will this person come inside and buy something?”

No column says that. The pump and the counter are two separate transactions, minutes apart, tied together only by the shopper id and the clock.

10:41

fuel, $43.10

→

10:46

coffee, $2.19

Same person, same store, five minutes apart, two rows that never meet. Our first job is to join them.

Defining the joint basket

We anchor a window on the fuel transaction and sweep up everything that shopper bought nearby.

−15 min

shopped, then filled

→

FUEL

the anchor

→

+45 min

filled, then shopped

Asymmetric on purpose: a few people buy first and fuel second, but most fuel first. Forty-five minutes after leaves room for a queue.

This is a unit of analysis decision, and it is ours to defend.

A trap in the label

First run said 19.4% of fuel visits bought something inside. The top category was SERVICES, at 40% of all conversions.

What SERVICES actually is

99.5% card-processing fees. debitfee, Debit Fee, Card Fee — $0.35, charged on the fuel sale itself.

The fuel transaction was leaking into its own label. A model trained on that learns “customers who pay by card buy SERVICES” — perfectly accurate, perfectly useless.

Look at what our positive class is made of before we train on it. Removing the fees moved the answer by seven points.

Example conclusions.

About 1 in 8. That is the number every model below has to beat, and the number that sets how much a discount can be worth.

What is a point worth?

Before building anything, price the ceiling. One point of attach rate, across the panel:

Per point of attach
Extra baskets, 2 years 361,426
Revenue at the $7.87 median $2.84M
Margin at an assumed 40% $1.14M
Per store, per year $605

Six hundred dollars a store. That is the whole in-store prize, and it is why a self-funded discount can never pay for itself — a conclusion we will reach again the hard way in Step 3.

Using the $11.84 mean basket instead gives $910. Margin is assumed — the data has no cost column. Example conclusions.

Step 3 · The models

Three questions, three models

The data told us something structural: whether they come in, what kind of thing they buy, and which exact item are driven by different signals.

Model A

Will they come inside?

Context + their own attach rate.

Model B

Which category?

The person, plus context.

Model C

Which SKU in it?

Who else buys like them.

All three want the customer id. They want different facts about the person: A their attach habit, B their category mix, C who else buys like them.

Is the split worth it?

P(category) = P(inside) × P(category | inside) is an identity, not a modelling choice. So we tested it — two stages against one direct model, same features, same split:

Category Two-stage One-stage Diff
Packaged Beverages 0.815 0.815 +0.000
Cigarettes 0.901 0.901 −0.000
Beer 0.895 0.893 −0.002
Candy 0.789 0.786 −0.003

The split buys no accuracy

Identical to three decimals. Anyone claiming a decomposition like this predicts better should be asked for this table.

What it does buy: roughly 3.4× cheaper training — B fits on the 13% who convert — plus an explicit P(inside) to gate the show-nothing call, and a failure you can locate. Engineering and communication, not accuracy.

Model A · Will they come inside?

Binary classifier over all 36.1M visits.

Features, all known at the pump

  • Hour, weekday, month
  • Store, fuel grade
  • Dollars pumped so far
  • Their own prior attach rate

Choices

  • GBTClassifier; logistic regression as the baseline
  • 12.8% positive — don’t rebalance
  • Calibrate it

Out-of-time AUC 0.703 → 0.827 when the attach rate goes in. For the 22% we have never seen, context is all there is — and they genuinely are harder.

Why we don’t rebalance

12.8% positive looks like class imbalance. The reflex is to resample to 50/50.

It would break the arithmetic

Trained 50/50, an average visit scores 0.5 instead of 0.128 — every expected value inflates about 4×, and we’d show discounts that lose money.

Do nothing

One in eight is not severe. A GBT handles it.

Or weight

weightCol changes the loss without touching the data.

Never SMOTE

It invents customers who don’t exist.

We aren’t classifying, we’re pricing. Accuracy doesn’t matter; calibration is the whole job.

Model B · What will they buy?

Six binary classifiers — one per category. Trained on the 4.6M converting visits.

Not a multiclass model

A basket holds several categories at once; our six shares sum to 119%, not 100%. A softmax would force them to compete and return numbers we can’t price.

Each model answers one question

P(beer | inside), P(coffee | inside), … — independently, so each is a real probability for its offer.

Where the person enters

Per category, from visits before this one: bought it before? what share of prior baskets? how long since?

GBTClassifier here — this is where interactions live. Beer on a Friday evening.

Does the customer id earn its place?

Fit every category twice — context alone, then context plus who they are — and compare on the held-out period:

Category Context + customer Gain
Cigarettes 0.667 0.884 +0.217
Other Tobacco / Nicotine 0.617 0.838 +0.221
Packaged Beverages 0.642 0.750 +0.109
Beer 0.764 0.870 +0.106
Salty Snacks 0.599 0.695 +0.096
Candy 0.643 0.726 +0.083

This is why A and B are separate models. Identity does nothing for whether they come in and almost everything for what they buy — most of all for the habitual categories.

Out-of-time AUC, 218,657 train / 78,259 test converting visits. Example conclusions.

Model C · Which SKU?

A category is not an offer. “Buy a beverage” sells nothing; “$1 off a 16oz Monster” does.

Category SKUs Top 100 covers
Packaged Beverages 4,049 45.1%
Candy 3,902 43.3%
Salty Snacks 2,681 51.9%
Cigarettes 1,311 85.9%

Now there is a catalog worth narrowing. Beverages has a long tail, so the store best-seller leaves most of the distribution on the table. Cigarettes does not — show the top seller and move on.

Decide per category by looking at the tail. Don’t apply one answer to all six.

The baseline to beat is the store’s own best seller — not random. Given those top-100 numbers it is a strong baseline, and ALS has to earn its place against it.

Why ALS works here

Earlier the objection was sparsity: the median shopper has two transactions. Conditioning on the category fixes that.

2.75M shoppers bought a beverage

55% bought three or more

16.2 / 7.6 purchases per distinct SKU, for those

Two purchases per SKU means people repeat items rather than sampling. That is a latent preference ALS can factor — implicit feedback, counts as confidence, not ratings.

It holds inside one person

That table could just be different people shopping at different times. So test the individual: take every pair of purchases by the same shopper.

Same-daypart pairs are 80% more likely to be the same item. Knowing the hour nearly doubles what we can say about a known shopper.

Putting the clock in the key

Item = SKU × daypart

“Red Bull@morning” is its own item. 4,049 → ~16k, still ~1,600 interactions each. Start here.

User = shopper × daypart

“Me in the morning.” Better semantics, but splits 2.75M users into 11M. Only for the heavy 20%.

Both — tensor factorization

The principled version. Not in Spark ML. Don’t.

Use three dayparts, not four — afternoon and night behave almost identically, so splitting them fragments the data for nothing:

Daypart Hours Share of visits Why it’s its own bucket
Morning 5am – 10am 21.8% The outlier. Energy drinks peak, colas bottom out
Midday 10am – 3pm 35.2% Sits between the two
Evening 3pm – 5am 43.1% Colas and Gatorade peak; afternoon and night match

Attach jumps from 9.8% to 13.2% at 5am and falls back after 11pm. That overnight tail is only 3.2% of visits, so it rides with evening rather than earning a fourth bucket.

Two ways to still get it wrong

It leaks quietly

Factor only on purchases before our scoring window. ALS feels like preprocessing, which is exactly why people forget — a model fit on all two years has already seen the answer.

Daypart is partly a proxy

Same-daypart pairs are often same-store, same-routine. Some of that 80% is “same mission”, not time. Fine to use — but we shouldn’t claim we measured a clock effect.

Going SKU-level is the revenue line

Brand-funded promotions — ZYN, Monster, Red Bull — are priced per SKU. A category model cannot spend that money.

ALS is a refinement on top

2× better than a best-seller list — but only for the 19% of shoppers with history in that category.

Build the best-seller fallback first: it is the primary path for four fills in five, not a degraded one.

Serving budget: all four stages must return inside the fill. The models are cheap; the risk is the history lookup, which argues for precomputing each shopper’s prior_* features nightly.

The decision rule

The naive version, and why it’s wrong:

P(buys) × margin − cost

This never goes negative while margin beats the discount. It also never asks the real question: what would have happened if we’d shown nothing?

The comparison that matters is between two worlds:

show the offer iff p₁ × (margin − discount) > p₀ × margin

p₀ = they buy anyway · p₁ = they buy when shown the offer

Show the math

Per 1,000 fuel visits at 2am. A packaged beverage averages $3.25 — assume 40% margin and a $0.90 discount, the going rate in our own discounts table.

No offer Offer shown
Buy 75 (7.5%) 90 (assume +1.5 pts)
Margin each $1.30 $0.40
Total $97.50 $36.00

−$61.50. We paid 90 people $0.90 each to win 15 extra buyers worth $1.30.

How wrong could the assumption be?

We assumed +1.5 points. Break-even needs p₁/p₀ > margin ÷ (margin − discount) = 3.25× — at 2am that is +16.9 points, not 1.5. The conclusion is not sensitive to the guess.

Margin and lift are assumptions — the data has no cost column. The $3.25 and the $0.90 are real.

It gets worse when business is good

Same offer at 6am, where attach runs 15.1%:

No offer Offer shown
Buy 151 166
Total $196.30 $66.40

−$129.90. The busy hour loses more, because the discount goes to more people who were already buying.

Traffic does not decide this

net = uplift × margin − (p₀ + uplift) × discount gets more negative as traffic rises. Under a constant relative lift, p₀ cancels out entirely. The margin-to-discount ratio decides, not the hour.

Funding is a dial, not a switch

The brand often pays most or all of the discount. Sometimes the retailer and the brand split it. That share decides everything:

our cost = discount × (1 − funded share)

Brand funds Our cost Effective margin Uplift needed to break even
100% $0.00 $1.30 any — margin per unit is untouched
75% $0.23 $1.07 +21%
50% $0.45 $0.85 +53%
25% $0.68 $0.62 +108%
0% $0.90 $0.40 +225%

At full funding we are not buying volume with margin — we keep the same margin per unit and simply sell more of them.

The model is a negotiating tool

Read the decision rule backwards and it answers a commercial question instead:

minimum funded share = 1 − (margin ÷ discount) × r ÷ (1 + r)

r = the relative lift we think the offer produces

p₀ cancels out. How likely this customer was to buy decides how much the deal is worth — not who should pay for it. The floor depends only on the margin-to-discount ratio and how far the offer moves people.

So we can walk into the brand conversation with a number: “at the lift we observe for Monster in the morning, we need you at 51% or better.”

negotiate.py prints that table per SKU and daypart.

What we cannot claim

Every number above is a prediction, not a causal effect. We have shown what people do, not what an offer would change.

The trap

Our best-scoring offer is usually the thing they were already going to buy. Discounting it just gives away margin.

What exists

The discounts table has real promotions — loyalty, mix & match, retailer promo — with observable outcomes.

Why it isn’t enough

Assignment wasn’t random. Stores promoted what they expected to sell, so the comparison is biased.

The ask for Murphy: a holdout. Some pumps show no offer. Without it, uplift is unmeasurable.

Could we measure it anyway?

The discounts table records redemptions, not impressions.

9,215,074 transactions with a discount applied

0 records of an offer shown and declined

P(buy | offered) has no denominator. But stores switch promotions on at different dates, and that stagger is a natural experiment:

Why not to believe it yet

Look at the store count

49 → 280 at week 0. Most stores were already running the promo when our data starts, so almost nobody has a pre-period.

The pre-trend is composition

The sample grows from 24 to 49 stores across those weeks. The rising line may be different stores entering the average, not behaviour changing.

Parallel trends fails

Share was climbing before the promo and plateaus after — the opposite of the shape a causal effect makes.

The design is sound. This estimate is not. Fixing it means a balanced panel of late adopters — a real project, and a better argument for the holdout than any methodology lecture.
