Baskets of Goods

Association rule mining on C-Store transactions

The question

What sells with what?


Every rule you find has to survive one question: what would the store do differently tomorrow?

Rules

A basket is the set of categories in one transaction. Duplicates collapse: two energy drinks count as one Packaged Beverages.

Three numbers describe a rule

For a rule A → B:

Support

How often A and B appear together, out of all baskets.

Is it common enough to care?

Confidence

Of baskets with A, how many also have B.

How reliable is the rule?

Lift

Confidence ÷ how often B shows up anyway.

Does A actually tell us anything?

Lift is the one that matters. High confidence on a very common item means nothing.

The arithmetic, on our data

Beer → Cigarettes, from the multi-item baskets:

Value Where it comes from
Support 0.065 6.5% of baskets have both
Confidence 0.318 31.8% of beer baskets also have cigarettes
Cigarettes alone 0.187 18.7% of all baskets have cigarettes
Lift 1.70 0.318 ÷ 0.187

Beer buyers are 1.7× more likely than average to also buy cigarettes.

Reading lift

Lift > 1

They go together more than chance. Worth acting on.

Lift ≈ 1

Independent. The rule tells you nothing.

Lift < 1

They repel. Buying A makes B less likely.

Association is not causation. A rule says the two co-occur, not that moving one moves the other. That’s what a test or a promotion proves.

Our data

What makes C-Store baskets different

Baskets are tiny

A drink. A pack of cigarettes. Fuel only. Support is low for everything.

A few categories dominate

Fuel, tobacco, lottery, and services swamp the counts.

SKUs are too fine

Hundreds of drink SKUs give sparse, unactionable rules. Roll up to category.

Time and place matter

Morning coffee baskets look nothing like Friday-evening beer baskets.

Profile first

2.7M line items in the Idaho sample

1.5M baskets, averaging 1.8 items

56% of baskets hold a single item

More than half the data carries no co-occurrence at all. Profile before you mine, or you’ll tune thresholds blind.

Cleaning: what counts as a basket

  • Single-item baskets: keep them and support collapses; drop them and every number is conditional on “basket had 2+ items.”
  • Fuel, tobacco, lottery: so common they attach to everything. Tag them, then decide whether they’re items or context.

Every one of these changes your rules. Write the decision down, and report your numbers next to it.

Roll up to category

At SKU level

20OZ MTN DEW → 44OZ REIGN

Support near zero. Thousands of rules, none actionable.

At category level

Packaged Beverages → Salty Snacks

Enough support to act on. A planogram decision.

The GTIN table gives you CATEGORY, SUBCATEGORY, BRAND, and PRODUCT_TYPE. Start at category, go finer only where a rule earns it.

Each layer is a table you can check. Don’t jump from raw to rules in one cell.

FP-Growth finds the rules

Spark’s FPGrowth does the counting. You give it baskets and two thresholds:

Minimum support

How rare an itemset can be before it’s ignored.

We used 0.5%, not the textbook 1%, because baskets are tiny.

Minimum confidence

How weak a rule can be before it’s dropped.

We used 5%, then sorted by lift.

It builds a tree of frequent items instead of rescanning for every combination, so it scales where the textbook Apriori algorithm stalls.

One more decision before you mine

We ran it on multi-item baskets only: 211,282 of 886,936.

  • Keep the single-item baskets and support collapses toward zero.
  • Drop them and every number becomes “out of baskets that had 2 or more items.”

Neither is wrong, but the denominator changes every result. Say which one you used, every time you report a lift.

Ask the class: which of these would you act on, and what would you do? Edible Grocery to Salty Snacks is a real adjacency decision. Fuel to Cigarettes at 1.05 is barely better than chance.

Segment to sharpen

silver = silver.withColumn("daypart",
    F.when(F.hour("DATE_TIME") < 10, "morning")
     .when(F.hour("DATE_TIME") < 15, "midday")
     .when(F.hour("DATE_TIME") < 20, "evening")
     .otherwise("late"))

morning = baskets_for(silver.filter(F.col("daypart") == "morning"))

Run the same mining per daypart, store, or chain. A rule that only holds at 7am is still a rule — and a better one, because it tells you when to act.

Takeaways

  1. Profile first. 56% single-item baskets changes everything downstream.
  2. Roll up to category. SKU-level rules don’t survive contact with a store manager.
  3. Lift, not confidence. Common items fake high confidence.
  4. Write down your cleaning decisions. They move the answer more than the algorithm does.
  5. Rules describe, models predict. The labeled version is its own deck.
