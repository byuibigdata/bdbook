One Customer, End to End

A real fill, traced through the data and the three models

pipeline v1 draws the reasoning. pipeline v2 draws the tables. This one follows a single customer from the moment the card is read to the offer on the screen, with the numbers the models actually produced.

Every figure below came out of explain.py, which prints this trace for any shopper:

uv run --with polars --with scikit-learn --with joblib --with implicit \
    python explain.py --demo

Example conclusions. A 25-store build of the PDI extract. The columns and the code are real; the margins are assumed, because the data has no cost column.

1 · A car pulls up

A card is dipped at the pump. It is read before the fuel flows, so identity arrives at the start of the fill, not the end. Everything here is known without touching a database:

shopper a37306b69b70… — hash of card BIN + last 4 + store state
clock 2026-06-15 07:12 → daypart morning
grade REGULAR
dollars pumped $38.82, live off the meter
store 8228

These are the gas features. Seven columns, no history, and they are all Model A needs.

2 · One lookup

A single read against a shopper-keyed table — precomputed nightly, not scanned live:

prior fuel visits 57
of those, went inside 52 (91%)
Category Bought before Share of prior visits
Packaged Beverages yes 86.0%
Cigarettes yes 36.8%
Candy yes 14.0%
Salty Snacks yes 7.0%
Other Tobacco / Nicotine no 0.0%
Beer no 0.0%

This is a regular, and a creature of habit: nine fills in ten end with a drink. Every figure is computed strictly from visits before this one — the window excludes the current row, which is the difference between a model that works and one that reads its own answer.

3 · Model A — will they come inside?

P(inside) = 0.0979        base rate across all visits 0.1411

Below average — 7:12 in the morning at this store is not a strong attach moment, and the model says so.

The number that moves this is the shopper’s own prior attach rate, not the number of visits behind it. Bucketed by visit count the attach rate is flat — 12.6% with no history, 12.5% with ten or more. Bucketed by their own attach rate it runs from 2.9% to 71.6%, a 25× spread, and adding it takes Model A from 0.703 to 0.827 out of time.

For a shopper we have never seen there is only context, and the model says so.

4 · Model B — which category?

Six independent binary models, each answering one question:

Category P(category given inside) Offerable?
Packaged Beverages 0.970 yes
Cigarettes 0.431 no — regulated
Candy 0.063 yes
Salty Snacks 0.031 yes
Other Tobacco / Nicotine 0.009 no — regulated
Beer 0.000 yes

Here the 57 visits do their work. An 86% historical beverage rate drives the score near the top of its range, and the two categories this shopper has never touched sit at zero. Note that cigarettes score 0.431 and are still not offered — that is a business rule, not a model output. See §7. Against context alone, knowing the customer is worth +0.08 to +0.22 AUC depending on the category — the single largest effect measured anywhere in this project.

Read saturated scores carefully. Isotonic calibration is a step function, so for a shopper whose history is emphatic enough it will return the top or bottom step — 1.0 or 0.0 exactly. Both are clamped (to 1 − 1e-5 and 1e-5) so a zero cannot wipe out the arithmetic downstream, but a score at either rail means “as confident as the calibrator can express”, not a certainty.

5 · Model C — which SKU?

This shopper is in the ALS factor matrix, so the ranking is personal rather than a best-seller list. That puts them in the minority: only 19% of next-period shoppers have enough category history to qualify.

Brand P(sku) Margin Brand funds Our cost
Monster 0.0075 $1.60 100% $0.00
Monster 0.0071 $1.68 100% $0.00
Mountain Dew 0.0037 $3.83 0% $0.90
Dr Pepper 0.0031 $4.30 0% $0.90

P(sku) is normalised within the category — “given they buy a drink, which drink” — which is what the decision rule multiplies against P(category).

Ranked by expected value, not by P(sku) — a thin margin on a likely item can lose to a fat margin on a less likely one, and a fully funded offer beats a self-funded one at equal probability because it costs us nothing.

6 · The decision

  P(inside)        0.0979
x P(category)      0.9697
x P(sku)           0.0075
  ------------------------
  P(buys this)     0.000708
x effective margin $1.60 - $0.00 = $1.60
  ------------------------
  expected value   $0.00114 per fill

Show: Monster.

Mountain Dew carries more than twice the margin, and still loses: it is self-funded, so $0.90 of that $3.83 is ours to give away.

A tenth of a cent sounds like nothing. Across 36.1M fuel visits it is not — and more to the point, it is positive, which a self-funded version of the same offer would not be.

7 · Five customers, one pump, one minute

One trace shows the mechanism. Five show what the customer id is actually worth. These are real shoppers picked to typify an archetype, then run through the identical context — same store, same 07:12, same REGULAR fill — so the only thing that varies is who they are.

personas.py picks and runs them.

What they did before

Persona Fuel visits Went inside Soda Cigs Salty Beer
Never bought 858 0% 0% 0% 0% 0%
Soda regular 16 100% 100% 0% 0% 0%
Cigarette regular 9 100% 0% 100% 0% 0%
Salty snack regular 13 100% 0% 0% 100% 0%
Beer regular 8 100% 0% 0% 0% 100%

The first one is worth pausing on: 858 fills, never once walked inside. That is a real customer type, and no amount of offer design changes it.

Model A separates them completely

Persona Prior attach P(inside)
Never bought 0% of 858 0.0000
Soda regular 100% of 16 0.8693
Cigarette regular 100% of 9 0.8320
Salty snack regular 100% of 13 0.8942
Beer regular 100% of 8 0.9291

A spread of 0.93, on identical context. The customer who has fuelled 858 times and never once walked inside is scored as close to zero as the calibrator can express — which is the correct answer, and a cheaper one than any offer.

Model B does

Persona Soda Cigs Salty Beer
Never bought 0.234 0.015 0.011 0.000
Soda regular 0.885 0.000 0.000 0.000
Cigarette regular 0.051 1.000 0.018 0.004
Salty snack regular 0.331 0.025 0.800 0.000
Beer regular 0.051 0.009 0.011 0.933

From 0.000 to 1.000 on cigarettes alone. Both models want the customer id, but different facts about them — A wants their overall attach habit, B wants their category mix. That is why they are separate models, not because one of them ignores identity.

And the offers differ

Persona Offer Expected value
Never bought Red Bull $0.00000
Soda regular Monster $0.01352
Cigarette regular Monster $0.00278
Salty snack regular Dot’s Pretzels $0.01311
Beer regular Modelo Especial $0.06906

Two of these deserve comment.

The cigarette regular is offered a drink. The model is 94.9% sure they want cigarettes and we will not show them — tobacco is excluded as a business rule, because it carries the highest margin and the shortest SKU tail, so expected value alone would push it at everyone, including the customer who has never bought anything. Targeted tobacco promotion is restricted in most jurisdictions. The system knows the right answer and is correctly forbidden from acting on it, so it falls back to the next thing it believes.

The never-bought customer is worth $0.00000. 858 fills, never once inside, and the model prices the offer at nothing. It still names a best guess — the screen has to show something — but the arithmetic says this is not a customer to spend a funded discount on. The beer regular, at $0.069, is worth roughly seventy times more than the five-cent average.

What the same trace says about the deal

Read backwards, the rule prices the negotiation:

If the offer lifts purchase by Brand must fund at least
+25% 64%
+50% 41%
+100% 11%

So: “At the lift we think this offer produces, we need you at roughly half.” The arithmetic has a useful property — the customer’s purchase probability cancels out. How likely they were to buy decides how much the deal is worth; the margin-to-discount ratio and the lift decide who should pay for it.

What this trace does not show

No p1. Every number is a prediction of what this customer does anyway, not of what the offer changes. The expected value is priced at p0 on both sides, which is why a self-funded discount can never win here. Fixing that needs a holdout — a slice of pumps that show nothing — and it does not exist.

No logged propensity. A production version would write what it showed and the probability it showed it with. Without that, no off-policy evaluation is possible later and the information cannot be reconstructed.

Assumed margins. unit_margin is average price times 40%. The PDI extract has no cost column, so the dollar figures move the moment someone supplies real ones.

Run it yourself

cd data/pipeline_analysis
UV="uv run --with polars --with scikit-learn --with joblib --with implicit"

$UV python explain.py --demo
$UV python explain.py --shopper <id> --time "2026-06-15 17:45" --grade DIESEL
$UV python explain.py --demo --json          # the same trace as a dict

serve.py gives the answer; explain.py gives the reasoning; negotiate.py gives the funding floor. All three read the same artifacts and are covered by the 50 checks in validate.py.
