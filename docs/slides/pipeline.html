Where the figures come from. One run over the PDI extract — 940 stores in eight Intermountain West states, October 2024 to September 2026. The models themselves are fitted on a 24-store pilot subset; exploration figures are full-estate, and the two are not mixed.

transaction-items-all

128,599,042 rows, one per item rung up, across 71.7M transactions and 940 stores.

pl.scan_parquet("transaction-items-all/*.parquet").select(
    "TRANSACTION_SET_ID",  # the basket key
    "STORE_ID", "DATE_TIME",
    "GTIN",                # barcode, or a placeholder when keyed
    "UNIT_QUANTITY", "GRAND_TOTAL_AMOUNT",
    "SCAN_TYPE",           # GTIN | NONSCAN | PLU | FMT_ERR
    "NONSCAN_CATEGORY",    # NACS dept for keyed lines — FUEL lives here
    "NONSCAN_SUBCATEGORY", # fuel grade lives here
)

58.9% of lines are scanned barcodes; the rest are keyed departments. Fuel is 28% of lines and $1.55B of $2.08B revenue.

shopper-id

71,790,254 rows, one per payment. Every transaction in this extract has a shopper — the unidentified ones were dropped before delivery.

shopper = (pl.scan_parquet("shopper-id/*.parquet")
    .select("TRANSACTION_SET_ID", "SHOPPER_ID")
    .unique(subset="TRANSACTION_SET_ID"))  # split tender → duplicate rows

SHOPPER_ID is hash(card BIN + last 4 + store state). Two consequences worth stating to a client:

  • The same card in two states is two shoppers, so cross-border travel is invisible.
  • BIN + last 4 is not unique to a card, so a few ids are more than one person.

8.0M shoppers, median 2 transactions each.

master-gtin

99,074 products, one row per barcode, 35 PDI categories.

gtin = (pl.scan_parquet("master-gtin/*.parquet")
    .select("GTIN", "CATEGORY", "SUBCATEGORY", "BRAND")
    .unique(subset="GTIN"))

Matches 88% of scanned lines. Two cautions:

  • CATEGORY is PDI’s taxonomy; NONSCAN_CATEGORY is NACS. Coalescing them mixes vocabularies.
  • Every row was last updated 2024-07-03, before our transaction window opens. Products launched since will never match.

Clean lines

Two filters and a coalesce. This is the whole of it:

lines = (items
    .filter((pl.col("UNIT_QUANTITY").cast(pl.Float64) > 0)
            & (pl.col("GRAND_TOTAL_AMOUNT").cast(pl.Float64) > 0))
    .join(gtin, on="GTIN", how="left")
    .with_columns(category=pl.coalesce("CATEGORY", "NONSCAN_CATEGORY"))
    .drop_nulls("category"))

The filters remove 481,568 voids and 701,406 returns. The coalesce labels 85.6% of lines; the rest are scanned items missing from the master.

Every money column is Decimal(38, x). Cast to Float64 on read or the arithmetic is slow and occasionally surprising.

Drop non-purchases

The rule is not “drop SERVICES”. It is drop anything the customer didn’t choose.

NOT_A_PURCHASE = ["FUEL", "SERVICES"]   # fuel is the anchor, not the outcome

SERVICES is 99.5% card-processing fees of about $0.35, charged on the fuel sale itself:

NONSCAN_DETAIL Lines Avg
CREDIT CARD FEE 3,940,565 $0.35
LABOR 5,030 $68.69
POST OFFICE 666 $21.05

Counting the fee as an in-store purchase leaks the fuel transaction into its own label. It inflated the attach rate by about seven points. A model trained on it learns “customers who pay by card buy SERVICES” — perfectly accurate, perfectly useless.

Fuel visit

The unit of analysis, and the decision a team has to defend. A visit is every purchase by the same shopper from 15 minutes before the fuel transaction to 45 minutes after.

BEFORE, AFTER = 15, 45

# Hour buckets keep the join small; the window spans at most three of them,
# so each candidate is offered to its bucket and both neighbours.
shifted = pl.concat([
    store.with_columns(bucket=pl.col("bucket") + pl.duration(hours=k))
    for k in (-1, 0, 1)])

delta = (pl.col("ts_store") - pl.col("ts")).dt.total_minutes()
hit = fuel.join(shifted, on=["SHOPPER_ID", "bucket"], how="left").filter(
    (delta >= -BEFORE) & (delta <= AFTER))

Asymmetric because most people fuel first. The timing says it’s generous, not tight:

Relative to the pump Share
−15 to 0 (shopped first) 16.2%
0 to 5 min 77.2%
5 to 15 min 5.3%
15 to 45 min 1.3%

Only 48,113 of 4.6M converting visits involve a second store, so dropping the same-store constraint costs nothing.

The label

36,142,617 fuel visits. 12.8% buy something inside. Median in-store spend when they do: $7.87.

basket = fuel.join(
    hit.group_by("fuel_tx").agg(n_store_tx=pl.col("store_tx").n_unique()),
    on="fuel_tx", how="left",
).with_columns(bought=pl.col("n_store_tx").fill_null(0) > 0)

This column does not exist in the data. We built it, and every number downstream inherits whatever we got wrong here. That is why the window and the SERVICES filter are worth a slide each.

About 1 in 8 — the base rate every model has to beat.

Context features

Everything knowable before the nozzle goes back. The card is read before the fuel flows, so identity is available too.

ctx = basket.with_columns(
    hour=pl.col("ts").dt.hour(),        # store wall-clock, per PDI
    weekday=pl.col("ts").dt.weekday(),
    month=pl.col("ts").dt.month(),
    # grade comes from NONSCAN_SUBCATEGORY: REGULAR, DIESEL, MID/PLUS, ...
)

DATE_TIME is documented as the wall clock time for the store, so dayparts need no timezone conversion — worth confirming, because the panel spans four time zones.

Signal strength, measured:

Feature Spread in attach
Hour 7.5% (2am) → 15.1% (6am)
Fuel grade 12.2% → 13.3% — almost nothing

A variable being available doesn’t make it useful.

History features

Per category, computed strictly from visits before the one being scored. This is the leakage rule with a clock on it.

from pyspark.sql import Window
w = (Window.partitionBy("SHOPPER_ID", "category")
          .orderBy("ts").rowsBetween(Window.unboundedPreceding, -1))

df = (df
  .withColumn("bought_before", F.max("bought").over(w))
  .withColumn("prior_share",   F.avg("bought").over(w))
  .withColumn("days_since",    F.datediff("ts", F.max("ts").over(w))))

rowsBetween(..., -1) is the whole trick — it excludes the current row.

History barely predicts whether they come inside (12.6% with none, 12.5% with 10+), but it is the best signal for what they buy. That asymmetry is why there are two models instead of one.

Category Relevance

Will they buy category X on this fill? Classification — six calibrated binary classifiers (HistGradientBoostingClassifier), one per category, each scoring every visit.

for cat in TOP6:                       # six independent binaries
    y = F.array_contains("cats", cat).cast("int")
    models[cat] = GBTClassifier(labelCol="y").fit(train.withColumn("y", y))

Not a multiclass model. A basket holds several categories at once, so a softmax would force them to compete and return numbers we cannot price.

Not rebalanced. The output is multiplied by dollars; a model trained on a balanced sample scores an average visit at 0.5 instead of its base rate and inflates every expected value about 4×.

The strongest single feature is the shopper’s own prior attach rate. How many times we have seen someone is worth +0.004 AUC; what share of those visits ended inside is worth +0.109.

SKU Affinity

Which item inside the category? Alternating least squares on implicit feedback — Hu, Koren & Volinsky (2008).

als = ALS(userCol="shopper_ix", itemCol="item_ix", ratingCol="n",
          implicitPrefs=True,        # counts are confidence, not ratings
          rank=32, regParam=0.05, alpha=20, coldStartStrategy="drop")

The item key is gtin@daypart. ALS takes no features, so the only way to give it a clock is to put one in the key — and the clock matters: same-daypart purchase pairs repeat the same SKU 18.0% of the time against 10.0% across dayparts.

SKU, not brand. At brand level ALS scores 0.88× popularity — worse than showing the best seller. With a handful of brands that matter there is nothing personal left to learn; the long tail of SKUs is what gives the model something to do.

Measured on the held-out period, over the fills where ALS actually ran and the customer bought inside the offered category:

hit@1 hit@10
ALS, SKU level 9.1% 34.3%
Store best seller 3.2% 16.9%
2.8× 2.0×

hit@1 is the operational number — the screen shows one offer. Over all fills, personalised and fallback together, it is 6.2% against 2.9%.

Scores are normalised within a category, so the number means given they buy this category, which item — which is what the decision multiplies against P(category).

This model only ever ranks inside one category. Category Relevance picks the category first, on probability alone, and SKU Affinity is confined to it. Ranking the whole catalog by expected value instead lets a narrow, high-margin category beat a wide, high-probability one on a technicality — its p_sku denominator is smaller — and offers candy to a customer we are 90.6% sure wants a beverage.

Best-Seller

The popularity ranking — and the path for about 68% of fills.

You enter the ALS factor matrix only by having bought in that category before the cutoff. Most fuel customers never did:

Covered by ALS
Scored fills in the held-out period 31.6%
…of the fills that converted in the offered category 54.9%

Measured, not assumed — validate.py fails if the published share drifts more than three points from what the scored table actually contains.

raw = popularity[daypart_items]        # no factors for this shopper

This is not a degraded path, it is the primary one. Build it first: the personalised tier is an improvement on top of a fallback you need anyway, and the vendor-funded revenue does not depend on ALS — it depends on going to SKU granularity at all.

Stage one · the category

Relevance decides this, on probability alone. Six calibrated models give six unconditional probabilities; we take the highest one we are allowed to show.

allowed = [c for c in categories
           if c not in NOT_OFFERABLE          # tobacco: a business rule
           and in_stock_and_offerable(c)]     # something to actually sell
category = max(allowed, key=lambda c: p_category[c])

Margin does not get a vote here. Let expected value pick the category and a narrow, high-margin category beats a wide, high-probability one on nothing but a shorter SKU tail — p_sku is normalised within a category, so a four-SKU category concentrates its probability where a three-hundred-SKU one spreads it thin. That is how a customer we are 90% sure wants a beverage ends up being offered candy.

Ties go to the daypart’s best seller, not to the alphabet. Before that rule a shopper with every category at the probability floor — someone who has fuelled a thousand times and never walked in — was shown beer, because “Beer” sorts first.

Stage two · the SKU

Rank the SKUs inside that one category. A SKU from any other category cannot appear, however profitable.

p_sku  = raw / raw.sum()          # normalised within the category
p_buys = p_category * p_sku       # P(buys this exact SKU on this fill)
order  = argsort(-p_buys)[:10]

Ranked on probability, not on expected value. The catalog’s margins are average price × an assumed 40% — the PDI extract has no cost column — so ranking on EV would let an assumption choose the item. The margin terms ride along for the funding conversation; they do not set the order.

in_stock and offerable filter the candidates; they do not change the denominator. P(sku | category) is a conditional over every SKU in the category, and excluding tobacco by business rule should not inflate what is left.

The same function serves the live fill and the batch table — scoring.py. Two copies of this ranking drifted twice, and both times the written table disagreed with the live answer.

Pair affinity

A different table answering a different question: not what will they buy, but what SECOND thing could a discount add to the basket.

What it is What it is for
cohort_index B’s rate among A-regulars ÷ the rate among equally frequent others Gating
excess the same comparison as a difference, in attach points Ranking
headroom 1 − this shopper’s own rate of B don’t discount what they already buy

Controlling exposure is the whole trick. Every cohort is defined as “at least five purchase baskets”, so every cohort is a cohort of heavy shoppers and indexes high on nearly everything. The first cut had drink regulars over-indexing on frozen food at 6.1× — pure visit frequency. Comparing against shoppers who buy just as often and do not buy the anchor divides that out.

Gate on the index, rank on the excess. Volume alone picks the popular item: Candy is 18.2% of every second item in a drink basket and looks obvious, but its index is 1.07 — not a complement, just everywhere. The index alone picks the tiny one.

The second item

The anchor is what they buy anyway, which makes it the one promotion certain to cost margin and change nothing. This is the offer worth funding.

Second category Their rate Equally frequent others Index Excess

Salty Snacks 12.2% 9.4% 1.30 2.8 pts

Alcohol is excluded on both sides as a policy, not a threshold: a beer anchor returns no second item, and alcohol is never offered as one. Beer’s only complement above 1.0 is Liquor at 1.08. Beer remains a legitimate anchor offer.

It ships as second_category and second_category_skus in the scored table, populated on 560,690 of 583,583 rows — empty where nothing clears the gate, which is a real answer rather than a miss.

Validation

Two models, two different questions, two different checks.

Stage one is judged on calibration, because its output is multiplied by dollars. A model that ranks well but is scaled wrong prices every offer wrong, and AUC would never show it. All 583,583 held-out fills, by decile of predicted P(beverage):

Decile Predicted Actual Gap
1 0.0001 0.0005 +0.0004
5 0.0385 0.0395 +0.0009
9 0.1400 0.1407 +0.0008
10 0.3430 0.3287 −0.0144

Calibrated to within a tenth of a point through the middle; mildly over-confident in the top decile, which is exactly where we spend.

Stage two is judged on whether it named the item they bought.

k SKU Affinity Store best seller Lift
1 9.1% 3.2% 2.8×
10 34.3% 16.9% 2.0×

Both rankings scored on the same rows — comparing ALS rows against fallback rows would compare two different populations. On fallback rows the two agree 100.000% of the time, which is the control that says the harness works.

validate.py — 116 checks. validate_sku.py prints the hit@k table.

Expected value

The naive form is wrong:

P(buys) × margin − cost        ← never negative while margin > discount

It never asks what would have happened with no offer. The real comparison is between two worlds:

show iff   p₁ × (margin − discount)  >  p₀ × margin

Worked, per 1,000 fuel visits at 2am. A beverage averages $3.25; assume 40% margin and a $0.90 discount, the going rate in your own data:

No offer Offer shown
Buy 75 90 (assume +1.5 pts)
Margin each $1.30 $0.40
Total $97.50 $36.00

−$61.50. And it gets worse at 6am (−$129.90), because the discount reaches more people who were already buying.

To break even you’d need to more than triple the purchase rate. A self-funded discount of this size cannot pay for itself out of that basket — at any hour. Which is precisely why every promotion in the data is a vendor program.

Margin and lift are assumptions; the data has no cost column. The $3.25 and $0.90 are real.

The pump screen

One offer, or none. The constraints the product has to live inside:

  • Fire during the fill. 77% of in-store purchases happen within five minutes of the fuel transaction.
  • 16.2% already shopped before fuelling. They are not addressable by a pump screen, so your real audience is the other 84%.
  • Ninety seconds, one screen, and a customer watching the gallons tick.

Showing nothing is a legitimate output. It needs no model to execute and it never loses money — which makes it the baseline every offer must beat.

Holdout

This does not exist, and it is the single thing worth asking Murphy for.

A slice of pumps that show no offer, assigned at random. Without it you can measure what people do, never what an offer changed.

# Random at the visit level, logged with the decision.
holdout = F.hash("fuel_tx") % 100 < 5     # 5% see nothing

Log the offer you showed and the probability you showed it with. Without logged propensities you cannot evaluate offline later, and that mistake is unrecoverable — the information was never captured.

With a holdout you can finally model uplift rather than propensity, and separate the persuadable customers from the ones who would have bought anyway.

Staggered adoption

The second-best design, available today — and the one whose assumptions our data violates.

Your discounts table records redemptions, not impressions: 9,215,074 transactions with a discount applied, and zero records of an offer shown and declined. P(buy | offered) has no denominator.

But stores switch promotions on at different dates:

Promotion Stores First Median Last
loyaltyDiscount 188 10/2024 01/2026 09/2026
Skupos 167 10/2024 11/2024 07/2026

Monster’s share of beverage lines around each store’s promo start:

Weeks Stores Share
−12 24 8.82%
−1 49 10.95%
0 280 11.55%
+12 273 11.79%

Don’t believe the +1.7 points. The store count jumps 49 → 280 at week 0, so almost nobody has a pre-period, and the apparent pre-trend is the sample growing from 24 to 49 stores rather than behaviour changing.

The design is sound; this estimate is not. A balanced panel of late adopters would fix it — and it is a better argument for the holdout than any amount of methodology.
